📖 Illustrated Handout — Immunological Mechanisms of Allergies

Material developed in line with the National Curriculum Guidelines for the Undergraduate Medicine Course (CNE/CES Resolution No. 3 of 30 September 2025). Application of international best teaching practices. Scientific Integrity Protocol applied.

Scientific Integrity Protocol — Statement: All information in this material was obtained directly from verified scientific sources (reference textbooks, peer-reviewed articles and guidelines from recognized scientific societies). Each factual statement is accompanied by a numbered citation that refers to the Audited References section at the end. The full text of CNE/CES Resolution No. 3/2025 (Medicine DCN) was consulted.

1. Introduction and Context

1.1 Why study the immunological mechanisms of allergies?

Allergic diseases are one of the greatest public health problems of the contemporary world. Hundreds of millions of people are estimated to be affected globally by some form of allergic disease, with a rising prevalence [1]. In Brazil, studies from the International Study of Asthma and Allergies in Childhood (ISAAC) have shown high prevalences of asthma, allergic rhinitis and atopic eczema in children and adolescents [2].

Understanding the immunological mechanisms underlying hypersensitivity reactions is essential for the physician, because it allows one to: (i) understand the pathophysiology of clinical presentations; (ii) rationally justify therapeutic options; (iii) recognize emergencies such as anaphylaxis early; and (iv) develop promotion, prevention and management actions within Primary Health Care (PHC) [3,4].

Alignment with the Medicine DCN (2025)
CNE/CES Resolution No. 3 of 30 September 2025 establishes the National Curriculum Guidelines for the Undergraduate Medicine Course, revoking CNE/CES Resolution No. 3/2014 [5]. The new framework establishes that the physician must be a professional with general, solid, critical and reflective training, able to work at all levels of health care, from promotion to rehabilitation, with care centered on the person, the family and the community and a broad understanding of the social determinants of health (Arts. 2–4). The new DCN lists 27 competencies (Art. 8, items I–XXVII), highlighting: comprehensive care with emphasis on primary care as care coordinator (item VI); evidence-based clinical and epidemiological reasoning (item III); evidence-based medicine and validated protocols (item X); social, environmental and climate-change determinants (item XV); the ethical use of digital technologies and AI in health (items VII and VIII); action in health emergencies (item XIV); professional self-care (item XVII); data protection (LGPD) (item XXVI); and equitable care for vulnerable populations (item XI).

1.2 Learning objectives

At the end of studying this handout, the student is expected to be able to:

  1. Describe (Remember/Understand) the Coombs and Gell classification of hypersensitivity reactions [6,7].
  2. Explain (Understand/Apply) the cellular and molecular mechanisms of each type of hypersensitivity.
  3. Analyze (Analyze) the inflammatory mediators and cytokines involved in the type 2 allergic response.
  4. Relate (Analyze/Evaluate) the immunological mechanisms to the clinical manifestations of the main allergic diseases.
  5. Apply (Apply/Create) this knowledge to the clinical approach of patients in the PHC setting, including the recognition and management of anaphylaxis [4,8].

2. Foundations of the Immune Response and the Concept of Hypersensitivity

2.1 The immune system: adaptation and regulation

The primary function of the immune system is to defend the organism against pathogens. Under normal conditions, this response is regulated, proportionate to the threat and self-limited. However, in some situations the immune system reacts in an exaggerated, inappropriate or prolonged way against antigens that would in principle be harmless (allergens) or against the body's own tissues (autoimmunity) [6,9].

2.2 Definition of hypersensitivity and allergy

Hypersensitivity is the term used to describe immune responses that cause tissue damage to the host. The term allergy, in its modern clinical sense, refers mainly to IgE-mediated hypersensitivity reactions (type I) [1,6].

Important terminological distinction: The World Allergy Organization (WAO) defines allergy as an "immune response mediated by specific mechanisms that leads to reproducible symptoms". A hypersensitivity reaction is a broader concept. Thus, every allergy is a hypersensitivity, but not every hypersensitivity is clinically called an allergy [1].

2.3 Coombs and Gell classification

In 1963, Philip Gell and Robert Coombs proposed a classification of hypersensitivity reactions into four types (I to IV), based on the type of immunity involved, the effector antibody(ies) or cell(s), and the time of onset of the reaction [6,7].

3. Type I Hypersensitivity (Immediate, IgE-Mediated)

3.1 Overview

Type I hypersensitivity is the most prevalent form of allergic reaction. It is mediated by immunoglobulin E (IgE), which sensitizes mast cells and basophils. After re-exposure to the allergen, these cells degranulate, releasing mediators that produce the characteristic symptoms within minutes [6,7,10].

3.2 Phases of the type I response

Sensitization phase (first contact — no symptoms)

  1. Antigen-presenting cells (dendritic cells, macrophages) capture and process the allergen, presenting it to naive CD4+ T lymphocytes [6].
  2. In predisposed individuals, under the influence of IL-4, naive T lymphocytes differentiate into T helper 2 (Th2) lymphocytes [6,11].
  3. Th2 cells secrete IL-4 and IL-13, which promote class switching of B lymphocytes to IgE production [6,11].
  4. IL-5, also produced by Th2 cells, stimulates the production and recruitment of eosinophils [6].
  5. The IgE produced binds to the high-affinity FcεRI receptors on the surface of mast cells and basophils. The individual is now sensitized [7,10].

Effector phase (re-exposure — immediate reaction)

  1. The allergen cross-links two or more IgE molecules already bound to FcεRI [7,10].
  2. This cross-linking triggers immediate degranulation, with release of preformed mediators and synthesis of new mediators [6,10].

3.4 Early and late responses

  • Immediate response: 5 to 30 minutes after exposure, mediated by histamine and leukotrienes. It presents as bronchoconstriction, vasodilation, edema and pruritus [6].
  • Late response: 2 to 24 hours after exposure, mediated by cytokines (IL-4, IL-5, IL-13) and eosinophil recruitment. It characterizes chronic inflammation [6,7].

3.5 Type 2 inflammation

The immune response in IgE-mediated allergic diseases fits within the concept of type 2 inflammation, characterized by the production of the cytokines IL-4, IL-5 and IL-13 by Th2 lymphocytes and type 2 innate lymphoid cells (ILC2) [11]. Epithelial cells, on recognizing allergens, release alarmins (TSLP, IL-25, IL-33), which activate ILC2 and amplify the Th2 cascade [11]. This concept underpins the development of biologics: omalizumab (anti-IgE), mepolizumab (anti-IL-5), dupilumab (anti-IL-4Rα) [8,12].

4. Type II Hypersensitivity (Cytotoxic)

In type II hypersensitivity, IgG or IgM antibodies bind to antigens on the surface of the body's own cells. This binding triggers cell destruction by three mechanisms: (1) complement activation (classical pathway → MAC → lysis); (2) ADCC (NK cells/macrophages recognize the antibody Fc); (3) opsonization/phagocytosis [6,7].

Examples: autoimmune hemolytic anemia, hemolytic disease of the newborn (Rh isoimmunization), immune thrombocytopenic purpura, ABO transfusion reactions [6].

5. Type III Hypersensitivity (Immune Complexes)

Antigen–antibody complexes (immune complexes) form and, under conditions of antigen excess, deposit in tissues (kidneys, joints, vessels, skin). The deposition activates complement, recruiting neutrophils that cause tissue damage. It manifests 3 to 8 hours after exposure [6,7].

Examples: serum sickness, immune-complex glomerulonephritides (post-streptococcal, lupus nephritis), hypersensitivity pneumonitis [6].

6. Type IV Hypersensitivity (Delayed, Cell-Mediated)

Type IV hypersensitivity is mediated by T cells (Th1, Th17, CTL), without involving antibodies. It manifests 24 to 72 hours after re-exposure [6,7,13]. The mechanism: APC → activation of sensitized T cell → cytokines (IFN-γ) → macrophages → inflammation [6,13].

7. Integrated View: Main Allergic Diseases

7.1 Allergic asthma

A chronic inflammatory disease of the airways, characterized by bronchial hyperresponsiveness and reversible obstruction. In its allergic form it involves the type 2 response: IgE, mast cells, eosinophils and Th2 cytokines [6,8]. GINA has incorporated the concept of type 2 inflammation for phenotypic stratification and for the indication of biologics [8,12].

7.2 Allergic rhinitis

One of the most common chronic diseases worldwide, IgE-mediated. It often co-occurs with asthma ("united airway"). Management: allergen avoidance, antihistamines, intranasal corticosteroids, immunotherapy [14].

7.3 Atopic dermatitis

Frequently the first manifestation of the "atopic march". It involves a type 2 (Th2) response and a skin barrier defect (filaggrin) [6,11].

7.4 Anaphylaxis

⚠ Emergency — Anaphylaxis: The most severe manifestation of type I hypersensitivity. A rapid-onset (minutes), systemic, potentially fatal reaction. Systemic release of mediators (histamine) from mast cells → intense vasodilation, airway edema, bronchoconstriction, hypotension and shock [4,15]. First-line treatment: intramuscular adrenaline (epinephrine) [4].

The diagnostic criteria (NIAID/FAAN, adopted by the WAO) include: skin/mucosal involvement OR respiratory compromise OR a drop in blood pressure, with rapid onset after exposure to a probable allergen [4,15]. The WAO (2020) reiterates that IM adrenaline is the first-line treatment and that its use is suboptimal worldwide [4].

8. Approach in the Public Health Context (PHC) in Brazil

8.1 Comprehensive care

Allergic diseases, because of their high prevalence and chronicity, are frequent in PHC. The Family Health Strategy (ESF) is a privileged setting for: reception, diagnosis and management of common conditions; health education; longitudinal follow-up; and timely referral [3,5].

8.2 Anaphylaxis action plan in the community

The WAO emphasizes patient and family education, the prescription of adrenaline auto-injectors and the preparation of an individualized action plan [4].

8.3 Asthma and rhinitis control programs

Management is based on clinical guidelines: inhaled corticosteroids as the basis of maintenance treatment (GINA), with medications available through the SUS pharmacy network [8]. Type 2 inflammation paves the way for the rational use of biologics in severe cases [8,12].

8.4 Social determinants, climate change and allergic diseases

The 2025 DCN (Art. 8, item XV) emphasizes that the physician must critically understand the social determinants of health and act on the impacts of climate change, environmental degradation and pollution [5]. Allergic diseases are directly affected: air pollution aggravates asthma and rhinitis; climate change alters pollen distribution; housing conditions (humidity, mold, dust mites) are perennial triggers, especially in vulnerable populations [5].

AI and digital technologies in health (DCN 2025, item VII): The new DCN emphasizes the critical and ethical use of artificial intelligence, telemedicine and data analysis in health [5]. In the management of allergic diseases: patient education (action-plan apps), treatment adherence, identification of epidemiological patterns and teleconsultation. The physician must exercise critical autonomy, preserving the doctor–patient relationship and confidentiality (LGPD, item XXVI) [5].

8.5 Programmatic assessment and active methodologies

The 2025 DCN (Art. 18) recommends active pedagogical methodologies that promote student protagonism, and (Art. 36) a programmatic, continuous and integrated assessment system, with mandatory individual feedback, covering three domains: cognitive (knowing/knowing how), psychomotor (demonstrating/performing) and attitudinal (being/relating to others) [5]. This material supports that approach: the handout works on the cognitive domain; the exercises and the case study stimulate application and analysis; and the clinical case, with small-group discussion, fosters attitudinal development.
