🏥 Clinical Case Study — Anaphylaxis and Asthma in Primary Care

Context: A case designed to apply knowledge of the immunological mechanisms of allergies in a realistic Primary Health Care setting in Brazil (ESF), aligned with the 2025 Medicine DCN (CNE/CES Res. No. 3/2025) [5], the WAO [4] and GINA [8]. It integrates diagnosis, immunological mechanism, acute management, chronic management and public health actions.

Part 1 — Case Presentation

Patient: J.S.S., female, 34 years old, homemaker, living in a peripheral neighborhood of a mid-sized city in Northeast Brazil. Seen at the neighborhood Basic Health Unit (UBS), linked to the Family Health Strategy.

History of present illness: J.S.S. comes to the UBS reporting that she was "stung by a wasp" in the backyard of her home about 40 minutes ago. She says "my whole body started itching", noticed "red welts" (urticaria), "my lips and eyes swelled up" (angioedema) and began to feel "shortness of breath with wheezing in my chest". She also reports "dizziness and darkened vision".

Past history: Asthma since childhood, with hospitalization at age 12; perennial allergic rhinitis; two previous episodes of intense local reaction after hymenopteran (wasp) stings, for which she did not seek care. Mother with asthma; sister with rhinitis. She does not take any regular medication. She says she "buys an inhaler at the pharmacy when an attack comes".

Clinical scenario: Rapid-onset presentation (minutes) after a wasp sting, with cutaneous, respiratory and cardiovascular involvement (hypotension).

Part 2 — Teaching Guide (for the instructor)

How to run the activity

The case should be worked in small groups (4–6 students), using an active methodology (problematization), in a session of approximately 90–120 minutes (DCN 2025, Art. 18 — active methodologies) [5]:

  1. Release the case in stages (Part 1, then questions, then solution) — the case is "sliced" to stimulate progressive reasoning.
  2. Guided discussion with key questions after each stage.
  3. Group discussion time: 15 min per block of questions.
  4. Plenary: each group presents its conclusions; the instructor contrasts them with the solution.
  5. Final synthesis: the instructor recaps the mechanisms, the management and the public health actions.

The case is deliberately challenging and contextualized: it addresses a real emergency (anaphylaxis) in a limited-resource setting (UBS), with coexisting asthma and atopy, requiring integration of immunological knowledge, clinical practice and public health.

Learning objectives of the case

  • Recognize the diagnostic criteria for anaphylaxis in a PHC setting.
  • Identify the immunological mechanism (type I, IgE-mediated hypersensitivity) and relate it to the clinical findings.
  • Apply first-line treatment (IM adrenaline) and justify it on pathophysiological grounds.
  • Plan chronic management: action plan, auto-injector, referral, immunotherapy.
  • Articulate public health actions in PHC (education, surveillance, comprehensive care).

Part 3 — Group Discussion Questions

Block A — Diagnosis and mechanism (15 min)

A1. What is the syndromic diagnosis in this case? Justify it based on the clinical criteria.

A2. Which type of hypersensitivity is involved, and what is the immunological mechanism step by step (from sensitization to clinical manifestation)?

A3. Why had the patient, who had already had intense local reactions before, not developed anaphylaxis in the previous episodes?

Block B — Acute management (15 min)

B1. What is the first therapeutic action to be taken immediately at the UBS? Specify the route, the approximate dose and the site of administration.

B2. Why are antihistamines and corticosteroids not the first measure? What is their role?

B3. At the UBS, without an advanced observation bed, what additional supportive measures are needed and what is the referral flow?

Block C — Chronic management and public health (15 min)

C1. After stabilization and referral, what are the follow-up recommendations according to the WAO (2020)?

C2. The patient has poorly controlled asthma. How can asthma management be integrated with post-anaphylaxis follow-up in PHC, according to GINA?

C3. What health education and surveillance actions can the ESF team carry out in the community?

Part 4 — Annotated Solution

Block A — Diagnosis and mechanism

A1. Diagnosis: ANAPHYLAXIS.

The patient shows rapid onset (minutes, within 40 min) after exposure to a known allergen (wasp sting). NIAID/FAAN criteria (adopted by the WAO): skin/mucosal involvement (urticaria + angioedema) + respiratory compromise (wheezing, dyspnea, SpO₂ 90%) + drop in BP (78/48 mmHg) [4,15]. Hypotension (systolic BP < 90 mmHg) constitutes anaphylactic shock — the severe form.

A2. Type of hypersensitivity: TYPE I (immediate, IgE-mediated).

Step-by-step mechanism:

Prior sensitization: in earlier episodes, wasp venom (phospholipase A2, hyaluronidase) was processed by APCs and presented to naive CD4+ T lymphocytes, which differentiated into Th2 [6]. Th2 cells produced IL-4 and IL-13, inducing class switching of B lymphocytes to anti-venom IgE [6,11]. The IgE bound to FcεRI on mast cells and basophils → patient sensitized [7,10].

Re-exposure (current event): the wasp venom cross-links multiple IgE molecules bound to FcεRI [7,10], triggering systemic mast cell degranulation, with massive release of mediators: histamine, leukotrienes, prostaglandins, PAF and cytokines [6]. The systemic (not local) release is what distinguishes anaphylaxis from a local reaction [4,6].

A3. Why anaphylaxis now and not before?

The type I allergic response is dose-dependent and depends on accumulated sensitization. The previous intense local reactions indicated that the patient was already sensitized, but the amount of venom injected was not enough to generate systemic mast cell activation. Repeated exposure tends to amplify the response (immunological memory booster) [6]. Thus, anaphylaxis can occur on a subsequent exposure. Crucial lesson: a previous intense local reaction is a risk factor for future anaphylaxis [4].

Block B — Acute management

B1. First action: INTRAMUSCULAR ADRENALINE (epinephrine).

Route: IM (not subcutaneous — IM absorption is faster and more predictable, especially with hypotension/edema) [4]. Site: anterolateral thigh (vastus lateralis) — not the buttock [4]. Adult dose: 0.3 to 0.5 mg of epinephrine (1 mg/mL = 1:1000), IM. May be repeated every 5–15 min if there is no response [4].

Adrenaline acts on α1 (vasoconstriction → ↑ BP), β1 (chronotropism/inotropism) and β2 (bronchodilation, mast cell stabilization) receptors [4]. This is the life-saving measure.

B2. Why are antihistamines and corticosteroids not the first measure?

Antihistamines block only histamine H1 receptors — they do not act on leukotrienes, prostaglandins or PAF, nor on hypotension. They are adjuncts for residual pruritus/urticaria [4].

Corticosteroids have an onset of action of hours (genomic effect) and do not reverse the acute phase. Their role is to prevent the late response and recurrence (biphasic reaction) [4].

Adrenaline is the only drug with rapid action on all components. The WAO warns that its use is suboptimal worldwide [4].

B3. Supportive measures and flow at the UBS.

MeasureRationale
IM adrenaline (first!)Reverses the condition [4]
Trendelenburg / supine with legs elevatedCounters hypotension through venous return [4]
Oxygen therapyCorrects hypoxemia (SpO₂ 90%) [4]
Fluid resuscitation (IV crystalloid, bolus)Vasodilation and capillary leak → relative hypovolemia [4]
Bronchodilator (salbutamol) after adrenalineAdjunct for refractory bronchospasm [4]
Antihistamine + corticosteroidAdjuncts — pruritus and prevention of the late response [4]

Flow: after initial stabilization (or concurrently), call SAMU/192 (emergency medical service) for monitored transport. Anaphylaxis requires observation (6–24 h) because of the risk of a biphasic reaction [4].

Block C — Chronic management and public health

C1. Post-anaphylaxis follow-up recommendations (WAO 2020) [4]:

  • Referral to allergology for etiological investigation (skin tests, specific IgE, BAT).
  • Trigger confirmation: wasp venom → evaluation for venom-specific immunotherapy (highly effective) [4].
  • Prescription of an adrenaline auto-injector and training.
  • Written action plan, in accessible language.
  • Education: avoid risk areas, wear protective clothing.
  • Identification card stating the risk of anaphylaxis.

C2. Integration with asthma management (GINA) [8]:

The patient has uncontrolled asthma (use of an "as-needed inhaler" = only a rescue bronchodilator, with no maintenance anti-inflammatory) — high risk of exacerbations. According to GINA, maintenance treatment should include an inhaled corticosteroid (ICS), with or without a LABA. The use of a SABA alone without ICS is discouraged [8]. Assess adherence, inhaler technique, triggers (coexisting rhinitis must be treated) and control (ACQ or ACT). Type 2 inflammation is the common substrate — in severe asthma uncontrolled despite ICS/LABA, refer for phenotyping and possible biologic therapy [8,12].

Summary of the case's lessons:
  • Anaphylaxis is a type I emergency and IM adrenaline is the first-line treatment — there is no justification for delaying it.
  • The mechanism is IgE-mediated: prior sensitization → cross-linking → systemic mast cell degranulation.
  • Previous intense local reactions are a risk factor for future anaphylaxis.
  • The coexistence of uncontrolled asthma worsens the condition and must be treated (ICS, per GINA).
  • PHC plays a central role in follow-up, education and comprehensive care, in line with the 2025 DCN and the SUS.
