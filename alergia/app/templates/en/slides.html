Immunological Mechanisms of Allergies

Pathophysiology, Classification and Clinical Approach

Lecture for Undergraduate Medicine • Primary Care/SUS context

In line with the Medicine DCN (CNE/CES Res. No. 3/2025) • International best teaching practices

Slide 3 — Fundamental Concepts

Hypersensitivity × Allergy

Hypersensitivity: an immune response that causes tissue damage, exaggerated/inappropriate [6].

Allergy (WAO): an immune response mediated by specific mechanisms that leads to reproducible symptoms [1].

Relationship: every allergy is a hypersensitivity, but not every hypersensitivity is clinically an "allergy".

A healthy immune system is regulated, proportionate and self-limited. In hypersensitivity, the response is exaggerated, inappropriate or prolonged [6,9].

Speaking notes:

Before we get into the classification, it is important to distinguish two concepts. Hypersensitivity is the broader concept — any immune response that causes damage. Allergy, in the modern clinical sense of the WAO, refers to responses mediated by specific mechanisms. In practice, rhinitis, allergic asthma and anaphylaxis fall within IgE-mediated allergy — type I.

Speaking notes:

In 1963, Gell and Coombs proposed this classification, which we still use today. The four types differ in three dimensions: the type of immunity (humoral in I, II and III; cellular in IV), the predominant effector, and the time to reaction. We will detail each one next.

Slide 5 — Type I: Phases of the IgE-Mediated Response

Sensitization phase (1st contact — no symptoms)

  • Allergen → APC → naive CD4+ T lymphocyte
  • Differentiation into Th2 (induced by IL-4) [6,11]
  • Th2 produces IL-4/IL-13 → B-cell class switching to IgE
  • Th2 produces IL-5 → eosinophils [6]
  • IgE binds to FcεRI on mast cells/basophils → sensitization [7,10]

Effector phase (re-exposure — symptoms within minutes)

  • Allergen cross-links IgE bound to FcεRI [7,10]
  • Degranulation → release of mediators [6,10]
Speaking notes:

Here is the heart of the lecture. On first exposure there are no symptoms — sensitization occurs. In predisposed people, the naive T lymphocyte differentiates into Th2, which produces IL-4 and IL-13 (class switching to IgE) and IL-5 (eosinophils). IgE binds to FcεRI on mast cells. On re-exposure, the allergen cross-links two or more IgE molecules, triggering degranulation. Note: it is the cross-linking that is the signal.

Slide 6 — Type I: Mediators and Late Response

GroupMediatorsEffects
PreformedHistamine, tryptase, heparinVasodilation, ↑ permeability, bronchoconstriction, pruritus [6]
LipidLeukotrienes, PGD2, PAFPotent bronchoconstriction, secretion [6]
CytokinesIL-4, IL-5, IL-13, TNF-αAmplification, chronic inflammation [6,11]

Immediate response: 5–30 min — mediated by histamine/leukotrienes [6]

Late response: 2–24 h — mediated by cytokines and eosinophils [6,7]

Therapeutic targets: antihistamines, leukotriene antagonists, corticosteroids, biologics (IL-4Rα, IL-5, IgE) [8,12]
Speaking notes:

The mediators fall into three groups. The preformed ones, such as histamine, cause the symptoms of the early response. The lipid ones, such as leukotrienes, cause potent bronchoconstriction. The cytokines sustain the late response, 2 to 24 hours later, and maintain chronic inflammation. That is why in persistent asthma we use corticosteroids and, in severe cases, biologics.

Slide 7 — Type II and Type III: A Quick Look

Type II — Cytotoxic

  • IgG/IgM antibodies bound to antigens on the cell surface [6]
  • Destruction by: complement (MAC), ADCC, opsonization [6]
  • Examples: autoimmune hemolytic anemia, hemolytic disease of the newborn, ABO transfusion reaction [6]

Type III — Immune Complexes

  • Ag–Ab complexes deposit in tissues → activate complement → recruit PMNs [6]
  • Time: 3–8 hours [6]
  • Examples: serum sickness, glomerulonephritides, lupus nephritis [6]
Speaking notes:

Types II and III also involve antibodies, but with different mechanisms. In type II, antibodies bind to antigens on the surface of cells and destroy the cell. In type III, immune complexes form and deposit in tissues, activate complement, recruit neutrophils and cause damage. Although they are not called "allergy" in everyday practice, they are essential for understanding immune-mediated diseases.

Slide 8 — Type IV: Delayed (Cell-Mediated) Hypersensitivity

  • Mediated by T cells (Th1, Th17, CTL) — no antibodies [6,7,13]
  • Time: 24–72 hours [6,13]
  • Mechanism: APC → activation of sensitized T cell → cytokines (IFN-γ) → macrophages → inflammation [6,13]
SubtypeCytokineExample
ContactIFN-γContact dermatitis (nickel) [13]
Tuberculin-typeIFN-γPPD test (tuberculosis) [6]
GranulomatousIFN-γ, TNF-αTuberculosis, sarcoidosis [6]
PHC application: The tuberculin test (PPD) — read at 48–72 h — is a type IV hypersensitivity. Fundamental for tuberculosis control in Brazil.
Speaking notes:

Type IV is the only cell-mediated one — it does not involve antibodies. It is mediated by previously sensitized T lymphocytes, which release interferon-gamma, recruiting macrophages. It takes 24 to 72 hours. The most relevant clinical application is the tuberculin test, the PPD: in someone exposed to the bacillus, the intradermal reaction produces an induration read at 48 to 72 hours.

Slide 9 — Integrating Concept: Type 2 Inflammation

Type 2 inflammation = a response mediated by Th2 and ILC2, characterized by IL-4, IL-5, IL-13 [11].

Epithelium exposed to the allergen releases alarmins (TSLP, IL-25, IL-33) → activate ILC2 → amplify Th2 [11]

Biologics: omalizumab (anti-IgE), mepolizumab (anti-IL-5), dupilumab (anti-IL-4Rα) [8,12]
Speaking notes:

This is a modern integrating concept: type 2 inflammation. IgE-mediated allergic diseases — asthma, rhinitis, atopic dermatitis — share a common substrate: IL-4, IL-5 and IL-13, produced by Th2 and ILC2. It all begins when the epithelium recognizes the allergen and releases alarmins. This is what underlies the biologics revolution. GINA has incorporated this concept.

Slide 10 — ⚠ Anaphylaxis: A Type I Emergency

Definition: a systemic type I hypersensitivity reaction of rapid onset (minutes), potentially fatal. Systemic release of mediators → vasodilation, airway edema, bronchoconstriction, hypotension, shock [4,15].

Diagnostic criteria (NIAID/FAAN, adopted by the WAO) [4,15]

  • Symptoms within minutes of exposure to a probable allergen AND one of the following:
  • Skin/mucosal involvement + respiratory compromise OR drop in BP
  • OR ≥ 2 of: skin/mucosa, respiratory, reduced BP, persistent GI symptoms
  • OR drop in BP after exposure to a known allergen
First-line treatment: INTRAMUSCULAR ADRENALINE (epinephrine) — anterolateral thigh [4]
Speaking notes:

Maximum attention here. Anaphylaxis is the most severe manifestation of type I hypersensitivity and it is an emergency. The NIAID/FAAN criteria, adopted by the WAO, help with recognition. First-line treatment is one and only one: intramuscular adrenaline, in the anterolateral thigh. Do not wait for antihistamines or corticosteroids as a first measure. The WAO warns that adrenaline use is suboptimal worldwide.

Slide 12 — Lecture Summary

Type I (minutes) — IgE, mast cells — Anaphylaxis, asthma, rhinitis

Type II (min–hours) — IgG/IgM, cell — Hemolysis

Type III (3–8 h) — Immune complexes — Serum sickness

Type IV (24–72 h) — T cells — Contact, PPD

Type 2 Inflammation (IL-4, IL-5, IL-13) — common substrate of allergies → target of biologics

Speaking notes:

To summarize: four types of hypersensitivity. Type I, immediate and IgE-mediated, is that of our classic allergies. Types II and III involve antibodies against cells or in complexes. Type IV is cell-mediated and delayed. And above it all, the modern concept of type 2 inflammation, the common substrate and the target of biologics.
